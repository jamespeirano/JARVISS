# Power, shelter and air

## Generator placement and carbon monoxide

Never run a fuel-burning generator in a house, garage, basement, shed or other enclosed space, even with doors or windows open. Place it outdoors more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents, directing exhaust away from occupied areas. An open garage door does not prevent carbon-monoxide poisoning. A location under an open kitchen window is unsuitable.

Use battery-operated or battery-backed carbon-monoxide detectors near sleeping areas. Keep the generator dry using the manufacturer's approved arrangement without moving it indoors or enclosing the exhaust. Do not handle wet electrical connections. If carbon monoxide is suspected, move everyone to fresh air without entering the contaminated space and seek reachable emergency help. Carbon monoxide has no dependable warning smell.

Sources: CDC, Generators and carbon monoxide; FEMA, Disaster preparedness manual.

## Cooking and heating in a tent or room

Do not burn charcoal or run an outdoor camp stove inside a tent, room or garage. An open flap or window does not make this safe. Carbon monoxide can accumulate, and flames add a fire risk. Keep combustion outside and away from openings; use equipment only in the conditions its manufacturer specifies.

For warmth, prioritize shelter from wind and rain, dry insulation and insulation underneath the body. Keep sleeping bags and bedding dry. Avoid treating a fuel-burning appliance as an improvised indoor heater. Keep a clear exit route and keep flames away from fabric, fuel storage and other combustible materials.

Sources: CDC, Generators and carbon monoxide; CDC, Hypothermia and frostbite; FEMA, Disaster preparedness manual.

## Smoke masks and gases

An N95 filters airborne particles when it fits and is used correctly. It does not remove carbon monoxide or other gases and vapors, and it does not supply oxygen. A particulate mask is not permission to enter a smoky, oxygen-deficient or chemically contaminated area. Leaving the exposure is the priority.

Fit matters: gaps, facial hair and unsuitable sizes reduce protection. Damaged, wet or dirty filtering facepieces may need replacement. Some breathing conditions make respirator use difficult. For wildfire smoke, reduce exposure and seek cleaner indoor air where available; no mask establishes that a building or route is safe.

Source: CDC NIOSH, Respirators and smoke.

## Battery and charger compatibility

Check the actual battery and charger labels: battery chemistry, nominal system voltage, permitted charging voltage/current, connector polarity and manufacturer compatibility. A plug that fits does not establish compatibility. A fixed-output 48 V lead-acid charger is not suitable for a 12 V lithium battery; both voltage and charging behavior are mismatched. Do not connect them to find out.

Use the charger specified or approved for the battery. Do not charge visibly swollen, damaged, leaking or overheating batteries. Keep terminals protected from short circuits. If a label or manual is missing, compatibility is unknown; save the exact manual in Docs rather than borrowing instructions for a different model.

Source: U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission battery-charging guidance; equipment labels and manufacturer manuals.

## Estimating battery runtime

Watt-hours measure energy. Watts measure power. Daily energy use is watts multiplied by hours of use per day. Runtime in days is usable watt-hours divided by daily watt-hours, assuming no recharging. For example, a 12 W radio used 2 hours daily consumes 24 Wh per day; 120 usable Wh would support 5 days under those assumptions.

If capacity is listed in amp-hours, watt-hours are approximately volts multiplied by amp-hours; voltage is essential. Actual usable energy may be below the nameplate rating because of battery condition, temperature, permitted discharge and conversion losses. Do not assume solar charging without a measured or justified input estimate. Plan → Power can track devices and an energy budget; replace estimates with observations when possible.

Source: Electrical unit definitions and JARVISS Power calculations.

## Equipment fault codes

Fault codes are model-specific. The same code can mean different things on two pumps, radios or chargers. Record the manufacturer, exact model and displayed message. Consult that exact manual; do not infer a meaning from a familiar code on another brand. If the model is already provided, the next useful request is the manual or relevant page, not the same model number again.

Before a permitted maintenance check, follow the manual's shutdown and isolation procedure. Do not assume a powered-off device has no stored energy or pressure. Preserve the exact ratings, units and warnings when explaining a label. A general troubleshooting overview cannot confirm a particular repair.

Source: Equipment-specific manufacturer manual; JARVISS document matching.

## Damp sleeping bags and cold ground

Moisture can come from your breath and perspiration even when rain stays outside. Avoid breathing into the sleeping bag. Change into dry clothes before sleeping and keep spare clothes and bedding protected from moisture. Use suitable insulation beneath you as well as over you; a shelter roof alone does not insulate against cold ground. Choose bedding for the expected temperature rather than assuming any sleeping bag is adequate.

Source: National Park Service, Camping: What to Bring, Shelter and Bedding.

## Sweating while walking in cold weather

Ventilate or remove clothing layers before you become sweaty. Protect dry layers and sleeping gear in waterproof bags, and add suitable rainwear before getting soaked. Adjust layers with activity; do not keep every layer tightly closed while overheating and then remain in damp clothing when you stop. Watch for stumbling, mumbling and loss of coordination in companions. Those signs need prompt attention and shelter.

Source: National Park Service, Noatak National Preserve, Hypothermia, prevention section (May 9, 2022).

## Choosing a shelter site

Choose ground away from dead or leaning trees, loose overhead limbs, rockfall, avalanche paths, drainage channels and flood marks. A dry streambed remains a drainage channel with flood risk; its being dry now does not make it a safe campsite. Do not camp beneath a hazardous tree because it appears stable at the moment. Consider the wind, rain, sun and expected temperature as well as access to materials. Low ground can collect cold air or water. Check for animals, insects and sharp objects before settling in.

Select a shelter you have the time, energy, tools and materials to complete before exposure becomes dangerous. A roof needs secure support and weather protection; the sleeping area also needs ground insulation and room for dry bedding. Recheck the structure and nearby hazards when wind or precipitation changes.

Source: U.S. Army ATP 3-50.21, Shelter and Clothing, paragraphs 6-9 to 6-12.

## Snow shelter ventilation

Do not seal a snow shelter airtight to make it warmer. Provide ventilation and keep the openings clear as snow accumulates. The Army chapter calls for a ventilation shaft and warns against closing vent holes. Ventilation also helps control moisture; melting and dripping can wet bedding and weaken a snow structure. Watch for blockage and changing conditions while occupied.

Keep fuel-burning stoves and heaters outside enclosed shelters. A vent hole does not establish that operating a stove inside a snow cave is safe. Older manuals describe cooking inside some snow shelters; current carbon-monoxide precautions in this guide take priority for choosing a cooking location. Snow construction also carries collapse, exposure and avalanche hazards; a text description cannot verify a shelter's strength or location.

Sources: U.S. Army ATP 3-50.21, Shelter and Clothing, paragraphs 6-25 to 6-27 and 6-39; CDC carbon-monoxide guidance.

## Floodwater and electrical panels

Stay out of standing water near electrical circuits, appliances or a breaker panel. Switch off the main power only when the switch can be reached safely from a dry location. When reaching it requires entering floodwater, stay clear and have a qualified electrician isolate the power. A stick, pole, household gloves or boots do not establish safe electrical isolation or make it safe to reach across floodwater to a panel. Keep others away from the area too.

Wet electrical equipment requires a qualified inspection before power is restored. Never turn power on or off while standing in water. If someone may still be touching a live electrical source, avoid touching them; a second person can also be shocked. Electrical disconnection and rescue around water or damaged power equipment require trained help.

Sources: CDC, What to Do to Protect Yourself From Electrical Hazards, reviewed August 26, 2026; CDC, Safety Guidelines: Reentering Your Flooded Home, August 24, 2026. The prohibition on improvised reach tools applies the requirement for safe dry access; it is not an electrical-work procedure.

## Gas smell and utility leaks

Leave a building with a suspected gas leak and move others away. Avoid flames, smoking, light switches and actions that could produce a spark. Do not stay inside to investigate the leak with electrical equipment. Seek reachable utility or emergency help from outside the affected area. Do not return until the building has been assessed as safe.

If natural gas has been shut off, only a trained technician should restore it. An outage does not make damaged pipework safe or provide a substitute for checking the system. Record and mark the hazard from a safe place so other people do not unknowingly enter it.

Sources: CDC, Safety Guidelines: Reentering Your Flooded Home; FEMA CERT Participant Manual, Unit 6, Utility Shutoffs.

## Fire growing beyond an extinguisher

Alert others and leave if a fire is growing, smoke threatens breathing, or the escape route is uncertain. A small extinguisher is for a small, contained fire when the operator is trained, can use it, is protected from toxic smoke and has a clear exit. Having a buddy or a dust mask does not make a smoke-filled room safe to enter. If these conditions are missing, retreat; do not gather equipment and return into smoke.

For an appropriate small fire, PASS means pull the pin, aim low at the base of the fire, squeeze the lever slowly and evenly, and sweep side to side. Check the extinguisher's class and instructions; not every agent is suitable for every fire. Keep the escape route behind you and leave if control is not immediate or conditions deteriorate. Alert reachable human help from outside; lack of phone service does not make it safe to stay.

Source: U.S. Fire Administration, Choosing and Using Fire Extinguishers, reviewed April 1, 2023; FEMA CERT Participant Manual, Unit 6.

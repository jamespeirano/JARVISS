# Working together after a disaster

## Organizing a small response team

Choose a coordinator and a known place for check-ins. Assess what happened, hazards, available people, skills and supplies before assigning jobs. Set a few priorities based on immediate needs and what the team can actually do. Give each assignment a clear task, person responsible, location, resources and reporting arrangement. Each person should know who they report to. Reassess as conditions change.

Use only the roles the situation needs. A neighborhood group does not need a separate person for every formal Incident Command System position. The FEMA manual describes planning, logistics and operations functions; a small group's coordinator may handle several functions until more people are available. Keep assignments within training and capability. Record tasks in Plan, and keep a paper copy when power is uncertain.

Source: FEMA CERT Participant Manual, Unit 2, CERT Organization, pages 2–5.

## Buddy system and accountability

Work with a buddy so someone can notice hazards, fatigue or injury and seek help. Know who entered an area, their assignment and when they should check back. A buddy pair does not make a hazardous structure safe to enter. FEMA describes search teams with a pair searching and a safety team remaining outside; the pair alone is not the whole support arrangement.

Agree on a way to signal trouble and a regrouping point. If radios or phones are unavailable, the plan must account for that before anyone separates. Check both people back in after a task. Record changes in location or assignment instead of relying on memory.

Sources: FEMA CERT Participant Manual, Unit 2, CERT Operations, page 5; Unit 7, Safety During Search and Rescue, page 3.

## Incident records and neighborhood team documentation

Write down what happened, where and when. Record access routes, hazards, damage, affected people, available resources, support locations and changing needs. Track which volunteers checked in, their skills, task assignments and whether they returned. Log actions and their results, communications, equipment use and handovers between shifts. Medical records need the person's condition and care provided, with privacy protected.

Identify who observed a fact and separate direct observations from another person's report or an estimate. Keep one shared situation record with dates and times so a later shift can understand it. Paper is useful; formal forms are not essential. In JARVISS, use Plan → Log for observations and reports, Tasks for assignments, People for skills and contacts, and Supplies for stock. Chat can discuss these records but does not save changes for you.

Source: FEMA CERT Participant Manual, Unit 2, Documentation and Documentation Flow, pages 12–13. JARVISS record locations are application guidance.

## Stress, burnout and repeated volunteer shifts

Plan rest, food, water and breaks rather than waiting for exhaustion. Rotate people between higher- and lower-stress duties when possible. A buddy can notice changes in concentration, mood or physical condition that the person overlooks. Reduce demands or replace a worker who cannot continue safely. Sleep and recovery matter after a shift as well as during it.

Offer support without requiring someone to recount distressing experiences. Let them choose whether and when to talk. Give practical help, make manageable plans and maintain connections with supportive people. Persistent or severe distress warrants reachable professional support. Do not use alcohol or pressure to keep someone working as substitutes for rest and care.

Source: FEMA CERT Participant Manual, Unit 5, Self-Care and Team Well-Being and Reducing Stress for Fellow CERT Volunteers, pages 4–7.

## Helping an upset survivor who does not want to talk

Introduce yourself, speak calmly and respect personal space. Listen if the person wants to speak; do not force an account of what happened or demand details. Ask about immediate practical needs and offer concrete help you can provide, such as water, a place to sit or contacting a companion through an available means. Respect privacy and choices.

People respond differently to the same event. Avoid judging their reaction or assuming that silence means they are fine. Give simple information you actually know, check that they understood it and acknowledge uncertainty where it exists. If a person is disoriented, injured or cannot care for themselves, attend to those immediate needs too.

Source: FEMA CERT Participant Manual, Unit 5, Working With Survivors' Emotional Needs, pages 8–12.

## False reassurance and promises that everything will be fine

Do not promise an outcome you cannot know. Statements such as “everything will be fine” can dismiss the person's fear and create false expectations. Acknowledge the difficulty, listen and explain what can actually happen next. For example: “I can stay with you while we work out where to shelter.” Only offer that if you can do it.

Practical, honest reassurance does not require predictions about rescue, restored utilities, missing people or recovery. Distinguish a plan from a confirmed result. Avoid claiming that you know exactly how the person feels.

Source: FEMA CERT Participant Manual, Unit 5, Working With Survivors' Emotional Needs, pages 8–12.

## Leaning walls, collapsed roofs and heavy structural damage

Stay out of heavily damaged buildings. Leaning walls, collapsed or partly collapsed roofs and floors, major structural cracks, and a building that has moved from its foundation are warning signs. A person calling from inside does not establish that an entry route will hold. Move away from the collapse area, keep others clear, and seek trained rescue assistance through an available safe means.

FEMA limits light search and rescue to suitable structures within the team's training and capabilities. Heavy damage is a boundary for civilian search teams, not a reason to hurry inside. If damage is uncertain, take the more cautious classification. Report what you observed and any known occupant location from outside without treating a guess as a confirmed fact. Structural stability cannot be certified through a chat description.

Source: FEMA CERT Participant Manual, Unit 7, Assessing Damage and Determining the Mission.

# Medicine storage and expiration

## What an expiration date tells you

A medicine's expiration date is supported by stability data for the product stored under its labeled conditions. It is not possible to calculate an unknown bottle's remaining potency from its age alone. Heat, moisture, freezing, light, damaged packaging and contamination can matter before the printed date. Normal appearance does not establish strength or sterility.

Keep the original packaging and label when possible. Record the exact name, strength, formulation, expiration, opening or mixing date and storage instructions. Do not relabel an expired product with an invented extension. If alternatives are limited, the uncertainty is product-specific; a generic claim that all old medicines are usable is not evidence about that medicine.

Source: FDA, Medicine expiration and stockpile studies.

## Why stockpile studies do not extend a household bottle's life

FDA's Shelf-Life Extension Program tests selected products and lots in federally controlled storage. An extension follows actual stability testing under defined conditions. It does not establish that every medicine lasts the same number of years or that an untested household bottle has the same potency. A study about tablets cannot automatically be applied to a mixed liquid, sterile injection or opened container.

Seek product- and lot-specific information when available. Save the relevant manufacturer label or official notice in Docs. Compare the exact formulation, packaging and storage history rather than only the drug name.

Source: FDA, Medicine expiration and stockpile studies.

## Opened, mixed and compounded medicines

Opening or mixing can start a shorter use period than the factory expiration. Follow the pharmacy's discard-after date and the product's storage instructions. Do not substitute the unopened box's expiration for that shorter date.

For example, an amoxicillin oral-suspension label specifies discarding remaining suspension after 14 days. That is an example for that product, not a rule for all antibiotics or all liquids. If your bottle says discard 14 days after mixing, use that instruction. Record when it was mixed so the date can be checked. Different preparations can require different temperatures and times.

Sources: FDA, Medicine expiration and stockpile studies; DailyMed, Amoxicillin for oral suspension, Hikma label.

## Insulin that froze or became too hot

Do not use insulin that has frozen, even after it thaws and looks normal. Freezing can damage it without a reliable visible sign. Keep insulin away from direct heat and sunlight, and prevent direct contact with ice or a freezer surface when cooling it.

Unopened insulin commonly requires refrigeration at 36–46°F (2–8°C), but the exact product label controls its storage and use limits. Heat exposure can reduce effectiveness. Do not assume a food-storage rule applies to medicine. Record the product, whether opened, time and temperature exposure, and seek a usable replacement and reachable clinical or pharmacy help. Do not change insulin type, concentration or dose merely to compensate for uncertain potency.

Source: FDA, Insulin storage during emergencies; exact product label.

## Insulin pens and vials have different use periods

There is no universal 28-day rule for every insulin. Check the exact brand, formulation, concentration and whether it is a pen or vial, opened or unopened. As one example, the TRESIBA label allows an in-use pen or vial up to 56 days under its stated temperature limits, and no later than its printed expiration. Other products have shorter limits.

If the exact current label says 56 days after opening, do not replace that with a generic 28-day answer. Record the opening date and follow all temperature conditions. A longer labeled period does not apply after freezing or other disqualifying storage damage. Keep a saved copy of the exact label available offline.

Sources: DailyMed, TRESIBA prescribing information; FDA, Insulin storage during emergencies.

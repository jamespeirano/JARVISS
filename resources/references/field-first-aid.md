# First actions for injury and illness

## Cuts and wounds after a disaster

Wash your hands with soap and clean water before wound care; use disposable gloves if available. Control bleeding with direct pressure first. After bleeding stops, gently rinse the wound with bottled or clean running water; saline is preferred if available. Clean around it with soap and clean water, pat dry and cover with a clean dressing. Inspect it again at least every 24 hours. Keep an open wound out of floodwater.

Do not close a dirty wound, bite or puncture over trapped contamination. Embedded material, animal bites, dirty punctures, spreading redness, swelling, drainage, increasing pain or fever need prompt reachable medical care. Confusion, trouble breathing or rapidly worsening illness make the situation more urgent. Cleaning a wound does not by itself establish tetanus protection.

Source: CDC, Emergency Wound Care After a Natural Disaster, October 26, 2017 factsheet; listed by CDC April 24, 2024.

## Tetanus prevention and dirty punctures

Tetanus prevention depends on wound care and vaccination history, not whether an object visibly looks rusty. Punctures, bites, wounds contaminated with soil, feces or saliva, and injuries with damaged tissue require assessment. Antibiotics or antibiotic ointment are not a substitute for tetanus vaccination or immune globulin when those are indicated.

Record what happened, when, contamination and the person's vaccine history. A clinician assesses vaccination and immune-globulin needs based on the wound, completed primary series, date of the last dose and immune status. Unknown vaccination history is not proof of protection. Continue cleaning and monitoring the wound while arranging reachable medical help; do not wait for symptoms to review protection.

Source: CDC, Clinical Guidance for Wound Management to Prevent Tetanus, September 8, 2026. This summary explains prevention; the linked source is intended for health-care providers.

## Heavy bleeding

First make sure you can reach the person without entering an ongoing hazard. Apply firm, steady direct pressure to the bleeding wound with clean cloth or gauze. Send someone for reachable trained help while pressure continues. Life-threatening bleeding needs prompt control; appropriate tourniquet use or wound packing may be needed depending on the wound location and training. Do not delay direct pressure to gather a long medical history.

Source: American Heart Association and American Red Cross, 2024 First Aid Guidelines. This short summary is not a substitute for practical training.

## Burns from a pan or hot water

Stop contact with the heat source and cool the burn promptly with cool running water. Do not apply butter, oil or ice. Remove nearby jewelry and loose clothing before swelling, but leave material stuck to the burn in place. Cover loosely with a clean, dry dressing or cloth. Do not deliberately break blisters.

Seek reachable medical help for a large, deep, electrical or chemical burn, a burn affecting breathing, or a burn involving the face, hands, genitals or a major joint. Cool the burned area while keeping the rest of the person warm. A burn may be more serious than its initial appearance suggests.

Sources: U.S. Fire Administration, Burn Prevention, First Aid and Calling 911; MedlinePlus, Treating minor burns.

## Unconscious or confused people and drinking

Do not put water, food or medicine into the mouth of an unconscious person or someone unable to swallow safely. They can choke or inhale it. Check responsiveness and normal breathing, and seek immediate reachable help. If not breathing normally, begin CPR according to your training and use an available AED. An unconscious person breathing normally still needs airway protection, monitoring and urgent help; position them according to first-aid training and suspected injuries. Do not leave them alone to get water.

Sources: CDC, Hypothermia and frostbite; American Heart Association and American Red Cross, 2024 First Aid Guidelines.

## Heat stroke: confusion in hot conditions

Confusion, slurred speech, collapse or loss of consciousness in a hot person can indicate heat stroke. The person may still be sweating. This is an emergency: move them out of the heat, seek urgent reachable help and start rapid cooling immediately. Remove excess clothing; use cold-water immersion when feasible and safely supported, or wet the skin and circulate air, with cold wet cloths or ice packs available for cooling. Keep monitoring breathing.

Do not force drinks into a confused, drowsy or unconscious person. Do not wait for a thermometer reading or for sweating to stop before acting. Heat exhaustion in an alert person who can swallow is different: rest in a cool place and sip cool fluids, with help for worsening symptoms or vomiting.

Sources: CDC, Heat illness; CDC, Heat-Related Illnesses: What to Look For and What to Do.

## Hypothermia: wet, cold and confused

Move to a safe warm, dry shelter first if one is available. Handle a severely cold person gently. Remove wet clothing and insulate from the ground with dry layers and blankets. Warm the central body—chest, neck, head and groin—gently. Warm nonalcoholic drinks may help only if the person is fully awake and can swallow safely. Never give drinks to an unconscious person.

Confusion, exhaustion, fumbling, slurred speech or drowsiness can be warning signs. Seek reachable trained help promptly. Alcohol is not a treatment. Do not rub frostbitten skin or warm numb tissue directly against a fire, heating pad or other intense heat. Protect it from further injury.

Source: CDC, Hypothermia and frostbite.

## Vomiting, diarrhea and dehydration

Use safe water and safe food, wash hands with soap, and separate food preparation from cleaning up illness. A person who is alert and can swallow should replace lost fluids. A packaged oral rehydration solution should be mixed with exactly the safe-water volume on its label; more concentrated is not better. Do not improvise a salt dose.

Seek reachable medical help for bloody diarrhea, diarrhea lasting more than 3 days, fever over 102°F (38.9°C), repeated vomiting that prevents drinking, or dehydration such as very little urine, dry mouth or dizziness on standing. Infants and medically vulnerable people can worsen quickly. Trouble swallowing, double vision or weakness after suspect preserved food needs urgent assessment.

Sources: CDC, Food poisoning symptoms; CDC, Hygiene after a disaster.

# Keeping food safe

## Food during a power outage

Keep refrigerator and freezer doors closed. An unopened refrigerator generally keeps food safe for 4 hours without power; a full freezer about 48 hours and a half-full freezer about 24 hours. These are estimates, not guarantees. Thermometer readings and the food's actual temperature history matter.

After 4 hours without power or another cold source, discard refrigerated perishables such as meat, fish, milk, eggs and leftovers. A normal smell or appearance does not prove safety. Do not taste to check. Food still containing ice crystals, or measured at 40°F (4°C) or below, can be refrozen or cooked; texture may suffer. If frozen perishable food has been above 40°F for more than 2 hours, discard it. Do not use the freezer's time estimate to override that limit.

Source: CDC, Food after a power outage or flood.

## Cooked rice, soup, meat and salads left warm

Discard cooked rice, meat soup, chicken salad and other perishable food left warm overnight. Reheating or boiling it again cannot reliably make it safe because some microbial toxins can remain. A normal smell does not change that decision.

The limit for perishable food left out is 2 hours, or 1 hour when the surrounding temperature exceeds 90°F (32°C). For example, chicken salad left for 90 minutes at 95°F exceeds the 1-hour limit: discard it. These limits also apply to cooked meat, egg dishes and cut produce requiring refrigeration. Refrigerating food after these limits have been exceeded does not undo unsafe storage.

Sources: FDA, Food storage and refrigeration; CDC, Food after a power outage or flood.

## Cooling freshly cooked food for storage

For freshly prepared food still within safe time limits, use shallow containers so cooked food cools quickly. Do not leave a large pot on the counter waiting for it to reach room temperature. If you cannot keep perishables cold, cook only what can be eaten within the safe time. Discard food that exceeds these limits even if it smells normal. Keep raw meat juices away from cooked and ready-to-eat food, and wash hands and utensils between tasks.

Sources: FDA, Food storage and refrigeration; CDC, Food after a power outage or flood.

## Refrigerator storage times

These times require prompt refrigeration and a fridge continuously at or below 40°F (4°C). They do not rescue food left warm, exposed to floodwater or otherwise contaminated. Follow a shorter product-specific limit when applicable.

- Cooked leftovers, cooked meat or poultry: 3–4 days.
- Soups and stews: 3–4 days.
- Prepared egg, chicken, ham, tuna or macaroni salads: 3–4 days.
- Raw chicken or turkey, whole or pieces: 1–2 days.
- Ground meat, ground poultry and raw sausage: 1–2 days.
- Raw beef, veal, lamb or pork steaks, chops and roasts: 3–5 days.
- Fresh finfish: 1–3 days.
- Opened deli or luncheon meat: 3–5 days; unopened package: 2 weeks.
- Opened hot dogs: 1 week; unopened package: 2 weeks.
- Bacon: 1 week.
- Raw eggs in their shells: 3–5 weeks; hard-cooked eggs: 1 week.
- Raw separated egg whites and yolks: 2–4 days.
- Pizza: 3–4 days.

Label containers with the preparation or opening date. A calendar reminder does not verify temperature control.

Source: FoodSafety.gov, Cold Food Storage Chart, reviewed September 19, 2023.

## Freezer storage and quality

Food kept continuously frozen at 0°F (-18°C) or below remains safe indefinitely; published freezer times describe best quality, not an automatic safety deadline. Freezing does not make spoiled food safe. Quality targets include cooked meat/poultry 2–6 months, soups/stews 2–3 months, raw ground meat/poultry 3–4 months, whole raw chicken/turkey 1 year and raw poultry pieces 9 months. Do not freeze eggs in their shells.

During an outage, decide using ice crystals and temperature history, not the original freezing date. Thaw perishables in a refrigerator, in cold water using appropriate sealed packaging and regular water changes, or using a microwave when food will be cooked immediately. Do not thaw meat on a warm counter.

Sources: FoodSafety.gov, Cold Food Storage Chart; FDA, Food storage and refrigeration.

## Cooking temperatures

Check the center or thickest part with a food thermometer, avoiding bone. Color and clear juices are not reliable tests.

- All poultry, including chicken, turkey and ground poultry: 165°F (74°C).
- Ground beef, pork, lamb, veal and goat: 160°F (71°C).
- Whole beef, pork, lamb and veal steaks, chops and roasts: 145°F (63°C), followed by a 3-minute rest.
- Casseroles and reheated leftovers: 165°F (74°C).
- Egg dishes: 160°F (71°C); whole eggs: cook until white and yolk are firm.
- Finfish: 145°F (63°C).

These cooking temperatures apply to food handled safely before cooking. Heating cannot reliably rescue food left warm too long or a swollen can. Clean the thermometer between uses.

Source: FoodSafety.gov, Safe Minimum Internal Temperatures, reviewed November 21, 2024.

## Food dates and shelf-stable supplies

Most food best-by or best-if-used-by dates describe quality, not a sudden safety cutoff. A date alone cannot establish safety: check the type of food, packaging, whether it was opened, storage conditions and signs of damage.

USDA quality-storage periods for unopened, intact commercially canned food kept cool and dry:
- Low-acid canned foods: plain beans, meat and most other vegetables — 2–5 years.
- High-acid canned foods: fruit and tomatoes — 12–18 months.
These periods describe quality, not guaranteed safety. They do not apply to damaged, heat-abused or home-canned food. Discard bulging, leaking or badly damaged cans; do not taste them. Once opened, use the applicable refrigerated-storage guidance.

Store dry staples in closed containers away from moisture, insects, rodents and chemicals. Rotate older usable food first. Check that no-cook foods and a manual can opener are available if fuel or power fails.

Sources: USDA FSIS, Shelf-stable food storage; FDA, Food storage and refrigeration; FEMA, Disaster preparedness manual.

## Infant formula use-by dates

Discard infant formula past its use-by date and obtain usable formula. Expired infant formula is unsuitable for feeding even when unopened. Formula is an exception to the general advice that many food dates describe quality. The manufacturer must ensure the product meets its labeled nutritional content and quality through that date. An unopened container, normal appearance or comparison with canned beans does not extend the formula's use-by date.

Source: USDA FSIS, Shelf-stable food storage.

## Food touched by floodwater

Discard food in packaging floodwater can enter: screw-top jars, snap lids, crimped caps, pull tops, cardboard, paper and home-canned foods. Washing the outside does not make their contents safe. Also discard food directly exposed to floodwater.

FDA describes a separate salvage process for undamaged, all-metal commercial cans and retort pouches. That exception does not apply to screw-top jars, damaged cans or swollen containers. If you cannot identify eligible packaging and complete the cleaning and sanitizing process, do not use it. Keep contaminated packaging away from clean preparation areas.

Source: FDA, Food and water after storms.

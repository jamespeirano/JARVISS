# Growing food

## Choose crops for the place and season

There is no planting date that works across the United States. Use local frost history, observed soil temperature, available daylight, water and the crop's needs. Frozen or waterlogged soil is not ready for warm-season seeds. Northern calendar dates should not be copied into southern or high-altitude gardens. Seed-packet days to harvest are estimates, and may count from transplanting rather than sowing.

Plan a mix of crops, not just one anticipated harvest. Record variety, sowing date, expected harvest window and what you actually harvest. Give beds enough light, drainage and room for access. Keep irrigation needs realistic for the available water. Start small enough to weed and water reliably; expanding an untended garden does not ensure more food.

Sources: USDA NRCS, Vegetable garden planning; University of Minnesota Extension, A Minnesota guide to garden timing.

## Soil, mulch and seed depth

Measure seed planting depth from the soil surface, not the top of a mulch layer. Follow the seed packet for that variety. Pull mulch aside where sowing; do not bury a small seed under several inches of mulch. Seed-depth guidance and mulch-depth guidance describe different things.

Use a sunny, well-drained site with known clean soil. A history of dumping, chemical contamination or flood deposits is reason to investigate before growing food there. Containers need drainage holes and suitable growing medium. Compost and soil tests can help plan fertility; adding more fertilizer indiscriminately can damage plants. Avoid herbicide-containing lawn fertilizer on vegetables.

Sources: USDA NRCS, Vegetable garden planning; University of Minnesota Extension crop guides.

## Tomatoes

Tomatoes need sun, warmth and a frost-free growing period. UMN recommends starting seed indoors roughly 5–6 weeks before local outdoor transplanting conditions; sow about 1/4 inch deep and provide strong light. Gradually acclimate seedlings outdoors before transplanting after frost risk has passed and soil has warmed. Space vining types about 2–3 feet apart and install supports at planting. Bush varieties need different spacing.

Water the soil consistently instead of wetting foliage; about an inch of rain or irrigation weekly is a starting point, adjusted to soil and weather. Excess nitrogen can produce leaves at the expense of fruit. Harvest as fruit ripens; gather suitable mature fruit before frost. Growth, yield and timing vary by variety and conditions.

Source: University of Minnesota Extension, Growing tomatoes in home gardens. Original short summary; regional calendar dates omitted.

## Common bush and pole beans

Sow common beans when soil has warmed; seeds can rot in cold ground. Plant approximately 1 inch deep, subject to seed-packet directions. UMN suggests about 4 inches between seeds and 2–3 feet between bush-bean rows. Set pole-bean supports before growth. Fava beans have different cool-season requirements.

Keep soil moist without waterlogging. Cultivate shallowly to avoid damaging roots. Pick snap beans before seeds cause prominent pod bulges. For dry beans, wait until pods and seeds dry fully; finish drying indoors when conditions require it. Store fully dried beans in a dry, dark place. Fresh beans require suitable preservation; plain home-canned beans need pressure canning.

Source: University of Minnesota Extension, Growing beans in home gardens. Original short summary.

## Potatoes

Start with disease-free seed tubers when possible. Grocery potatoes may carry disease or have sprout-suppressing treatment. A cut seed piece needs an eye; let the cut surface dry and heal before planting. In warmed, well-drained soil, UMN suggests pieces about 3–5 inches deep and 10–12 inches apart, with rows 30–36 inches apart.

Hill soil around growing stems to keep tubers covered from light. Keep moisture reasonably consistent. New potatoes can be dug earlier; mature storage potatoes are harvested after the plants dry and skins mature. Avoid damaging tubers during harvest; use damaged ones first instead of storing them. Cure and store suitable potatoes in dark conditions. Do not eat sprouts or extensively green potatoes.

Source: University of Minnesota Extension, Growing potatoes in home gardens. Original short summary.

## Radishes

Radishes are a cool-season crop. Use loose, well-drained soil with ample light. Sow roughly 1/4–1/2 inch deep and 1–2 inches apart, then thin to the variety's needs, commonly 2–4 inches. Keep soil evenly moist rather than saturated. Too much nitrogen encourages leaves rather than roots.

Small spring varieties may mature in about 25–35 days under suitable conditions; storage and daikon varieties differ. Harvest before roots become woody, split or overly hot. Heat and delayed harvest can reduce quality. Small successive sowings spread the harvest instead of producing everything on one day.

Source: University of Maryland Extension, Growing Radishes in a Home Garden. Original short summary.

## Basil

Basil is frost-sensitive: do not sow it outside into frozen soil. Sow after frost danger has passed, in a sunny, well-drained site. UMN recommends about 1/4 inch seed depth and thinning to 6–12 inches between plants. A 2–3 inch mulch layer is not the seed planting depth. Basil needs roughly 6–8 hours of bright light daily.

An indoor start is possible if warmth and adequate light are available. Pinch or cut stems above leaves to encourage new growth; regular harvest delays flowering and woody growth. Containers dry faster than garden beds. Leaves can be dried or frozen for storage, but homemade herb oil is not safe for room-temperature storage.

Source: University of Minnesota Extension, Growing basil in home gardens. Original short summary.

## Watering and keeping a useful garden record

Inspect the soil below its dry-looking surface before deciding when to water. Observe wilting, drainage and weather together. Container plants and sandy soils often dry faster than deep beds. Water the root zone; do not assume a universal daily schedule works for every crop.

In Plan → Garden, record what was planted, the variety, its expected harvest range and the next check. Record seed failure, pests, watering, frost and actual yield. Keep observed harvest separate from predicted harvest when planning food supplies. Save regional extension guides or seed-packet text in Docs so later questions can use information specific to the crop and location.

Sources: USDA NRCS, Vegetable garden planning; JARVISS Garden record fields.

# Hygiene and waste

## Handwashing with stored water

Wet hands with clean running water, apply soap and scrub every surface for at least 20 seconds, including between fingers, under nails and backs of hands. Rinse with clean water and dry with a clean towel or air-dry. Wash before handling food, after toilet use and after cleaning waste or caring for someone who is ill.

If soap and water are unavailable, an alcohol-based hand sanitizer containing at least 60% alcohol is an alternative for many situations. It does not remove every contaminant or replace washing visibly dirty hands. Keep a handwashing station separate from food and waste handling. A controlled-flow container can help conserve stored water while allowing rinsing.

Source: CDC, Handwashing; CDC, Hygiene after a disaster.

## Backed-up sewers or flooded septic systems

Do not flush a toilet into a backed-up sewer or flooded septic system, including by pouring in a bucket of water. Adding water can increase the backup and spread contamination. Stop unnecessary discharge into affected drains and keep people away from sewage. Do not enter a septic tank; dangerous gases and oxygen deficiency can be fatal.

Use an available portable toilet or a contained temporary sanitation arrangement with a disposal plan appropriate to local conditions. Keep human waste away from drinking-water collection, food preparation, children and animals. Wash hands after handling waste. Do not dump sewage into a stream, well, storm drain or saturated ground near water sources. Flooded systems need assessment before reuse; do not assume a falling water level proves the system is undamaged.

Sources: EPA, Septic Systems—What to Do after the Flood; CDC, Hygiene after a disaster.

## Cleaning with bleach

Never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners. Dangerous gases can form. Use the product's instructions for the surface and purpose, with ventilation and suitable gloves and eye protection. A drinking-water dose, a water-container sanitizing mixture and a surface-cleaning mixture are different; do not substitute one for another.

Remove visible dirt before applying an appropriate disinfectant. Keep cleaning products in labeled containers away from food and drinking water. If products are mixed accidentally and fumes appear, leave the area for fresh air and seek reachable help. Do not add another chemical to try to neutralize the mixture.

Source: CDC, Guidelines for Cleaning Safely After a Disaster.

## Separating clean and contaminated areas

Mark where treated drinking water and clean food are kept. Keep waste containers, dirty cleaning equipment and raw meat preparation away from those areas. Use separate utensils or clean and sanitize them between uses. After flooding, do not assume a well or a container is safe because the water looks clear.

Keep wound dressings and medical supplies dry and clean. Record illness in the group, who needs help and which water or food sources were shared; observations can help identify a problem but do not diagnose its cause. Assign waste and cleaning work explicitly so it continues when people are tired or roles change.

Sources: CDC, Hygiene after a disaster; FEMA, Disaster preparedness manual.

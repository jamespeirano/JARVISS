# Drinking water

## Choosing a water source

A mapped river, stream, spring, well or drinking fountain is a recorded location, not a water-quality test. Running water can contain pathogens. A fountain may have no supply during an outage. Collecting water also requires a safe approach and permission where applicable. Prefer known safe stored water. Keep untreated and treated containers separate.

Do not drink water suspected of containing fuel, pesticides, industrial chemicals or radioactive contamination. Boiling, household bleach and ordinary camping filters cannot establish that it is safe. Use a different source. Never use water from a heating-system radiator or boiler for drinking or cooking.

Source: CDC, Making drinking water safe.

## Boiling creek, river or lake water

For water with a microbial risk and no suspected chemical contamination: if cloudy, let sediment settle or strain through a clean cloth, paper towel or coffee filter, then pour off the clear water. Straining alone does not disinfect it.

Bring clear water to a rolling boil. Keep it boiling for 1 minute at elevations up to 6,500 feet. Above 6,500 feet, keep it boiling for 3 minutes. Start timing once it reaches a rolling boil. Let it cool, then store it in a clean, sanitized container with a tight cover. Do not add untreated water or ice to cool it. Boiling does not remove fuel or toxic chemicals.

Source: CDC, Making drinking water safe.

## Disinfecting drinking water with bleach

First read the label: use regular, unscented household chlorine bleach with a known sodium-hypochlorite concentration. Follow any drinking-water instructions on that label. Do not use scented bleach, color-safe bleach or products with additional cleaners. Confirm the water volume and bleach concentration before measuring a dose. A container-cleaning solution is much stronger than a drinking-water dose; never confuse them.

For bleach containing 5%–9% sodium hypochlorite, CDC's clear-water amounts are:
- 1 quart or liter of water: 2 drops, or 0.1 mL.
- 1 US gallon of water: 8 drops, or 0.5 mL.
- 5 US gallons of water: 40 drops, or 2.5 mL.

If water is cloudy, murky, colored or very cold, use twice those amounts. First settle or strain cloudy water and draw off the clearer water. Stir well and wait at least 30 minutes before drinking. Store covered in a sanitized container. These amounts apply only to 5%–9% bleach. Do not guess for another concentration. Disinfection does not make chemically contaminated water safe and is less effective than boiling against some parasites.

Source: CDC, Making drinking water safe.

## Filters, tablets and iodine

A typical portable filter does not remove viruses. An absolute pore size of 1 micron or smaller can remove parasites; 0.3 micron or smaller is needed for bacteria. A nominal rating is not the same as an absolute rating. Follow the actual filter's instructions and use a suitable disinfection step for organisms it does not remove. A coffee filter only removes particles.

Use water-treatment tablets for the volume and contact time on their exact label. Chlorine or iodine tablets do not reliably kill Cryptosporidium; correctly used chlorine-dioxide products can. Pregnant people, people with thyroid problems and people sensitive to iodine should avoid iodine-treated water. Iodine is also unsuitable for long-term routine use. Boiling is an alternative when fuel and a suitable vessel are available. UV devices require clear water and the manufacturer's instructions.

Source: CDC, Making drinking water safe.

## Choosing a safe drinking-water container

Use a durable container with a tight lid that previously held food or drinking water. Never repurpose a pesticide, fuel, bleach or other toxic-chemical container, even after rinsing. A narrow opening or tap helps avoid dipping hands and dirty utensils into safe water.

Source: CDC, Storing drinking water.

## Cleaning an empty food-safe storage container

These cleaning steps are for a container already suitable for drinking water. They cannot make a pesticide, fuel or other toxic-chemical container suitable.

For sanitizing an empty container, wash with soap and water and rinse first. CDC specifies unscented bleach containing 5%–9% sodium hypochlorite: mix 5 mL (1 teaspoon) into 1 liter of water, or 19 mL (4 teaspoons) into 1 US gallon. Close and shake so this solution touches every inside surface. Wait at least 30 seconds, then pour the solution out. Air-dry or rinse with safe water before filling. This is a container-cleaning solution, not drinking water.

Fill with safe water, close tightly and label with treatment date. Store cool, out of direct sun and away from fuels and pesticides.

Source: CDC, Storing drinking water.

## Planning a water supply

FEMA's starting storage target is at least 1 US gallon per person per day for drinking and sanitation. Heat, activity, illness, pregnancy, nursing, pets and cooking can increase needs. This is a planning quantity, not a reason to ration everyone to an identical drinking allowance.

Count usable safe water, people and daily use separately. If use is per person, multiply by the number of people before dividing stock by daily use. Include washing and food preparation when estimating the total. Recheck remaining stock each day and record a backup source and treatment method. Avoid depending on an untested source simply because it appears on a map.

Source: FEMA, Disaster preparedness manual; JARVISS planning arithmetic.

# Navigation and obstacles

## Back azimuth: the opposite compass bearing

A back azimuth points in the opposite direction from the original bearing. For bearings in degrees, add 180 to a bearing below 180; subtract 180 from a bearing of 180 or more. Normalize 360 to 0. This is not 360 minus the original bearing. Examples: 40 degrees becomes 220; 220 becomes 40; 0 becomes 180; 180 becomes 0. Both bearings must use the same north reference. Degrees and mils are different units.

An opposite bearing alone does not retrace bends around obstacles or establish a safe route. Keep track of your actual route and recognizable landmarks. Check arithmetic before moving.

Source: U.S. Army, Determine a Back Azimuth, December 6, 2024, performance steps.

## True north, grid north and magnetic north

True north points toward the geographic North Pole along a meridian. Grid north follows the north-south lines of a map's rectangular grid. Magnetic north is the direction indicated by a magnetic compass at that place and time. Grid north and true north are not generally identical.

The declination diagram on a topographic map identifies these references. Magnetic declination is the difference between true and magnetic north; the grid-magnetic angle relates grid and magnetic north. A bearing measured against map grid lines needs the appropriate grid-to-magnetic conversion before use with an unadjusted magnetic compass. Follow that map's diagram and your compass instructions. Do not apply an adjustment twice to a compass already adjusted for it. An old map's magnetic value can be out of date; the angle depends on location and date.

Sources: USGS, What do the different north arrows on a USGS topographic map mean?; USGS, Method #1 for using a compass.

## Keeping your route when walking around an obstacle

On suitable ground, a rectangular detour preserves the original line: turn 90 degrees aside and measure that sideways distance; turn to the original bearing and continue beyond the obstacle; turn 90 degrees toward the original line and travel the same sideways distance back; then resume the original bearing. Merely turning back to the original bearing after the first sideways leg leaves you on a parallel line, not the original one.

Track sideways legs separately from progress toward the destination. Check bearing arithmetic and confirm your location against recognizable terrain. This explains the geometry, not a route through unseen ground. Choose a different plan if the detour would enter a cliff, fast water or other hazard. Compass and pacing errors accumulate, so use checkpoints rather than relying on one long unverified leg.

Source: U.S. Army ATP 3-50.21, Navigation, paragraph 7-57, handling obstacles and detours.

## Measuring a pace count

Walk a known measured distance at your normal walking pace, counting consistently each time the same foot lands. Repeat the measurement and calculate your average count for that distance. Record the distance and method along with the result; a count without its measured distance is not useful.

Pace length changes with slopes, footing, wind, fatigue, load and clothing. Measure under relevant conditions and expect error, rather than copying somebody else's count. A counted distance is an estimate. Use identifiable checkpoints and terrain to check your position as you travel.

Source: U.S. Army ATP 3-50.21, Navigation, pace-count methods, page 7-27.

## Contour lines and steep slopes

Contour lines join points at the same elevation. The contour interval is the elevation difference between adjacent contour lines; read it from that map. Closely spaced lines indicate a steeper slope, while widely spaced lines indicate gentler ground. A short map distance across tightly packed contours can mean difficult terrain rather than a shortcut. A contour map describes terrain, not current footing, snow, flooding or whether a bridge remains usable.

Source: U.S. Army ATP 3-50.21, Navigation chapter. See the illustrated navigation PDF in Docs for terrain diagrams.

## Fast water across a trail

Stop before entering fast-moving water across a trail. Turn back or wait for safer conditions; a line on the map does not establish that a crossing is safe. Depth and speed can be hard to judge from shore. Do not assume a previously used ford or mapped bridge is usable now. Search for a verified safe alternative from firm ground rather than testing the current with your body.

If someone falls into swift water, keep them in sight without entering the current yourself. A reachable branch, throw rope or throw bag may help from a stable position. Seek available trained help. Entering the current can create a second casualty. Access to a phone or rescuers is not assumed here.

Source: National Park Service, Rocky Mountain National Park, Swift Water Safety, July 14, 2025.

## Assessing a backcountry stream crossing

Be willing to turn back or delay. Water level, current, cold, footing, weather and the abilities of every person matter. A crossing that works for one person can exceed another's ability. Conditions may change through the day and after rain. Keep dry clothing and essential supplies protected; a fall can leave a person cold and without critical equipment. Do not infer an acceptable depth or speed from an AI answer alone.

The Army navigation chapter describes historical crossing techniques, including techniques for people already in water. Those descriptions do not establish that attempting a particular crossing is appropriate. Evaluate the actual obstacle before committing to it.

Source: National Park Service, Alaska, River Crossing; U.S. Army ATP 3-50.21, Navigation chapter.

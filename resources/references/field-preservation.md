# Preserving food

## Choose a method you can complete

Before starting, check the food, equipment, clean water, fuel, packaging and storage conditions. Refrigerating, freezing, drying and canning have different requirements. A freezer requires dependable power; canning requires a tested process and the right equipment; drying requires controlled conditions and suitable storage. Save an exact tested recipe and equipment manual offline before relying on a method. A general overview is not a processing-time table.

Use sound ingredients. Preservation cannot repair food that has already spoiled or spent too long at unsafe temperatures. Label the batch, method and date. Plan what happens after a container is opened, particularly if no refrigeration is available.

Sources: CDC, Home canning and botulism; USDA FSIS, Shelf-stable food storage.

## Low-acid vegetables, beans, meat and fish

For fresh plain green beans intended for room-temperature jar storage, use pressure canning with a tested recipe. Plain green beans are low-acid food. Low-acid foods, generally above pH 4.6, require pressure canning; this also includes most other vegetables, meat, poultry, fish and seafood. A boiling-water bath does not provide the required protection against botulism. An electric multicooker with a canning button is not a substitute for a suitable pressure canner.

Use the exact tested recipe for food, packing method, jar size, processing time, pressure and altitude. Do not invent a time by scaling another recipe. A lid can seal without adequate processing, so a sealed lid is not evidence of safety. If food was hot-filled without the required process, do not treat it as shelf-stable. If equipment or a validated recipe is missing, choose a method you can complete safely rather than improvising a canning process.

Source: CDC, Home canning and botulism.

## Leaking, swollen or suspect jars and cans

Do not taste food from a leaking, bulging or swollen container. Do not try to rescue it by boiling longer, recanning or checking the smell afterward. Discard it. Botulinum toxin may have no smell, taste or visible warning. Food of unknown or incorrect canning history also should not be eaten.

CDC's advice to boil certain correctly prepared home-canned foods before serving is an additional precaution, not permission to salvage suspect containers. Keep suspect food away from children, animals, utensils and clean food. If anyone ate it and develops trouble swallowing, double or blurred vision, slurred speech, weakness or breathing trouble, seek urgent reachable medical help.

Sources: CDC, Home canning and botulism; CDC, Food poisoning symptoms.

## Jerky and dehydrating meat

For home jerky, USDA recommends heating meat to 160°F (71°C), or poultry to 165°F (74°C), before dehydrating. Then use a dehydrator that maintains 130–140°F (54–60°C) through drying, following a tested process. A 130°F dehydrator alone is not the initial pathogen-killing step. Drying or smoking until the surface looks done is not proof of safety.

Use a thermometer, clean handling and the correct procedure. USDA lists 1–2 months for properly prepared home-dried jerky; commercially packaged products can have different limits. Storage time is not a guarantee for inadequately heated or incompletely dried meat. Do not substitute a campfire approximation for a measured process.

Source: USDA FSIS, Jerky.

## Herbs in oil and foil-wrapped potatoes

Keep homemade garlic or herb oils refrigerated and discard unused portions after 4 days. Do not store homemade herb oil at room temperature as a shelf-stable preserve.

Keep cooked foil-wrapped potatoes at 140°F (60°C) or hotter until served, or refrigerate them with the foil loosened so air can reach them. Leaving a wrapped potato warm for hours is not safe storage. Observe the ordinary perishable-food time limits too.

Source: CDC, Home canning and botulism.
